A few things stand out:

Insurance is one of the biggest lines, an average {{stat:bud_insurance}} of the budget. Sponsors' budgets are based on quotes at the time of filing; buyers should expect the first renewal to come in higher.

Water and sewer is bigger than most buyers expect, an average {{stat:bud_water}}. NYC DEP bills the building, not the unit, and rates rise nearly every year.

Management fees are worth reading. The average management line in these budgets is about {{stat:bud_fee}} a year, or roughly {{stat:bud_fee_unit}} per unit, an average {{stat:bud_mgmt}} of the budget. In {{stat:bud_sponsor_mgr}} of these plans the sponsor manages the building itself during the first year, and {{stat:bud_sponsor_nofee}} of those {{stat:bud_sponsor_n}} record no fee for it. That looks like a saving; it isn't. The budget will jump when the board hires a real manager. The managing agents directory shows which firms manage NYC condos.

The reserve line is often decorative. The budgeted reserve or contingency is an average {{stat:bud_reserve}}, but the actual funded reserve at closing is a separate item. Where a plan states a reserve fund amount, the average is only about {{stat:bud_reserve_mean}}, and {{stat:bud_reserve_none}} of those plans put it at zero. Look for the working capital fund and the reserve fund in Part I of the plan, not just Schedule B.

What's Usually Missing from a First-Year Budget

Schedule B is a sponsor's estimate, prepared to make the common charges look reasonable. Common omissions:

  • Real property taxes on the units (paid by owners, not the condo, so correctly excluded, but easy to forget when comparing to a co-op)
  • Local Law 11 façade work, roof replacement, boiler replacement (capital, not operating)
  • Staff salary increases after the first union contract
  • Insurance at renewal rather than at filing

Some plans include a certification from a managing agent or accountant that the budget is reasonable. Some don't. The plan says which.

How to Read a Schedule B

Schedule B is organized by section: Income (common charges, sometimes commercial or parking income), then Expenses, then a total. If there's a commercial or garage unit, there may be separate sub-budgets. The footnotes explain the assumptions behind each line, and that's where the real information is: the insurance quote, the management contract, whether the super lives on site.

Budgets change after closing. Later budgets are filed as amendments under the same plan ID; see how to find and read offering plan amendments.

Common Questions

What is Schedule B in a condo offering plan?

Schedule B is the sponsor's projected budget for the condominium's first year of operation: expected income from common charges and any commercial or parking units, each expense line, and footnotes explaining the assumptions.

What is the biggest expense in a NYC condo budget?

In recent plans the biggest lines are insurance (an average {{stat:bud_insurance}} of the budget), water and sewer ({{stat:bud_water}}), staff and cleaning where the building has them ({{stat:bud_staff}}) and management ({{stat:bud_mgmt}}).

Is the Schedule B budget guaranteed?

No. It is a projection prepared by the sponsor at filing. Insurance renewals, staff contracts and the switch from free sponsor management to a paid managing agent commonly raise costs after the first year.

Where do I find a condo's reserve fund?

Look in Part I of the offering plan for the working capital fund and the reserve fund. The contingency line in Schedule B is a budget item, not the funded reserve.
